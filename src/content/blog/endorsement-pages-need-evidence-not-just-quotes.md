Endorsements are easy to underuse.

Most websites handle them the same way: add a headshot, paste the quote, list the person's title, and move on.

That is better than hiding the endorsement, but it leaves a lot of value on the table. A strong endorsement is not just a compliment. It is third-party evidence. The page should help the visitor understand what the endorsement proves.

That matters because people rarely read every word with equal attention. They scan. They look for names they recognize, claims that feel specific, and reasons to trust the organization before they commit more time.

The design has to support that behavior.

## Pull Out The Proof

A good endorsement usually contains more than one message.

It might speak to fairness, reliability, character, professionalism, experience, responsiveness, community involvement, or results. If the entire quote is presented as one block of text, those points can blur together.

The better move is to preserve the full quote while also extracting the most important ideas into short proof points.

For example, an endorsement might say someone is fair, consistent, deeply involved in the community, and trusted in a high-responsibility role. Those ideas should not be buried. They can become small highlighted statements around the quote:

- Fair process
- Community commitment
- Proven judgment
- Trusted leadership

That does not change the endorsement. It makes the meaning easier to see.

## Keep The Original Quote Intact

There is one important line not to cross: do not rewrite a person's endorsement unless you have permission.

Even if the submitted wording has awkward grammar, a missing word, or a phrase you would personally tighten, the safest public version is the exact quote as provided. You can add surrounding context. You can design around it. You can highlight themes. But changing the words can create approval problems, especially when the endorser's name and title carry real weight.

This is where design becomes useful. The full quote can remain untouched, while the page around it does the clarification work.

## Give Endorsements A Real Home

Endorsements should not only live in a small carousel or a forgotten section near the bottom of a homepage.

If endorsements are strategically important, they deserve a dedicated page that can grow over time. That page should have:

1. A clear navigation path from the header.
2. A featured endorsement area for the strongest current proof.
3. Reusable cards for additional endorsements.
4. Anchor links so individual endorsements can be shared directly.
5. A layout that can handle one endorsement today and many later.

That last point is easy to miss. The first endorsement page should already be designed for the tenth endorsement. Otherwise each new quote turns into a mini redesign.

## The Takeaway

An endorsement page is not a storage area for nice words.

It is a trust-building page.

The job is to preserve the endorser's exact statement, make the strongest proof easy to scan, and give the website a repeatable structure that can grow as more endorsements arrive.

Quotes create credibility.

Evidence design turns that credibility into momentum.
