Ad reporting usually fails at the handoff.

The agency has the numbers. The platforms have the exports. The client has a credit card statement, a memory of what was promised, and a reasonable question: what am I approving?

That question deserves more than a spreadsheet attachment.

For recurring local advertising work, a better pattern is a client-facing approval page with a built-in audit trail. It should show the campaign totals, explain which charges were already paid directly to the platform, identify which charges need reimbursement, calculate the management fee, and put the results in plain-English context.

The goal is not to make reporting prettier. The goal is to reduce confusion before money changes hands.

## Separate Platform Spend From Agency Fees

The first rule is to split money into the right buckets.

Ad platforms, especially Google and Meta, often bill in different ways. Sometimes the client card pays the platform directly. Sometimes the agency fronts the spend and needs reimbursement. Sometimes one channel is direct-billed while another is reimbursable.

If those lines get blended, the final total may be mathematically correct but operationally confusing.

A clean approval page should show:

- Platform spend paid by the client
- Platform spend paid by the agency
- Reimbursement due to the agency
- Management fee percentage
- Management fee dollar amount
- Total amount due now

That structure prevents a common trust problem: the client thinking they are being charged twice for media they already paid for.

## Show The Math In Public

Management fees should never feel hidden inside a total.

If the agreement is 20 percent, show the 20 percent. If the fee is based on all media spend, say that. If it only applies to certain channels, say that too. Then show the formula in a way a business owner can verify in ten seconds.

For example:

- Ad spend: $1,000
- Management fee: 20 percent
- Fee: $200
- Reimbursable spend: $300
- Amount due: $500

This is not over-explaining. It is good operations.

Clear math turns a billing conversation into an approval workflow.

## Add Performance Context, Not Spin

A spend review should also answer whether the campaigns behaved reasonably.

That does not mean pretending every result is perfect. It means comparing the numbers to the campaign's actual goal and to practical benchmarks for the business type.

For a local restaurant, for instance, high-intent actions such as direction requests, calls, menu visits, and local search engagement matter more than vanity impressions alone. A low cost per local action can justify spend even when the campaign is not producing a traditional lead form. A broad awareness campaign can be useful, but it needs different expectations: reach, frequency, CPM, click activity, and market coverage.

The approval page should make that distinction obvious.

One section can explain direct-response performance. Another can explain awareness performance. A third can call out caveats, such as limited budgets, incomplete months, or platform attribution differences.

The tone matters. Do not bury weak spots, but do not judge a campaign by the wrong metric either.

## Keep The Approval Page Stable

The page itself becomes part of the record.

That means it should be index-safe when it contains client-specific billing details. It should use a private or noindex route. It should avoid public blog-style language. It should be written for the client who needs to approve the charge, not for a search engine.

It should also leave room for the next step: a payment link after internal approval. That sequencing matters. First verify the numbers and explanation. Then add the payment action. Billing pages should not pressure a client to pay before the agency is confident the math and story are right.

## The Takeaway

Ad spend reporting is not only a marketing task. It is an operations task.

The strongest client review pages combine finance, platform data, benchmark context, and approval flow in one place. They make the spend defensible, the fee transparent, and the payment request easier to trust.

When the numbers are clear before the invoice goes out, everybody has a better conversation.
